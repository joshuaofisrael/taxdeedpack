Florida tax deed due diligence checklist: how to research a property before the auction

By the Tax Deed Pack research team · Updated October 26, 2026

Florida tax deed sales are sold as is. The clerk runs the auction, but the homework on the parcel is yours. This checklist walks through the twelve things to verify before you place a bid, with each legal point tied to Florida Statutes chapter 197 or an official county source.

Why research matters more at a tax deed sale

When a tax certificate holder applies for a tax deed, the clerk advertises the parcel and sells it at public auction to the highest bidder, either in person or online.1 Clerks generally make no promises about title or condition. Charlotte County’s clerk, for example, tells bidders it is highly recommended to research properties before sale day so they understand what they are buying, including liens, zoning and property restrictions.5 Your bid is final once you win, and the deposit is nonrefundable, so the time to find a problem is before the auction.

The 12 step checklist

  1. Confirm the parcel identity. Match the parcel ID, legal description and tax deed number on the clerk’s sale list to the property appraiser record. Florida parcels are sold on the legal description, not on a street address, and some lists show no address at all.
  2. Map it. Pull the parcel up on the county GIS map. Check its shape, acreage, road frontage and whether it is a sliver, a retention pond, a common area or a landlocked lot.
  3. Check legal access. A parcel with no road frontage and no recorded easement can be very hard to use or resell. Look for easements in the recorded documents.
  4. Read the property appraiser card. Note land use code, improvements, year built, assessed value and whether the parcel is assessed as homestead. On homestead property the opening bid must include one half of the assessed value.2
  5. Read the tax deed file. Ask the clerk for the file by tax deed number. It typically contains the property information report and the list of parties who were sent notice under section 197.522, which shows who held recorded interests.6
  6. Search for government liens. Most rights and interests end when the tax deed issues, but a lien of record held by a municipal or county governmental unit, special district or community development district survives if it is not paid from the sale proceeds.3 Search official records by owner name and parcel for code enforcement, demolition, lot clearing, utility and special assessment liens.
  7. Look for federal tax liens. Search for any IRS lien against the owner of record and check whether the IRS was noticed. How a federal lien is treated after a tax deed sale depends on federal law and the facts. NOT VERIFIED here; confirm with a professional.
  8. Check zoning, flood zone and wetlands. Use the county zoning map and the FEMA flood map. A cheap lot that cannot be built on may be worth far less than it looks.
  9. Check for an association. Search the chain of title for a recorded declaration. Even where older unpaid dues do not carry over, assessments after you take title will be yours.
  10. Drive by, do not trespass. Until the deed issues it is someone else’s property. A drive by can show occupancy, condition, access and whether a neighbor is using the land.
  11. Plan for redemption and cancellation. The owner or another party can redeem the certificate any time before the tax deed is issued, unless full payment for the deed has already been made to the clerk.4 Expect parcels to drop off the list, sometimes on sale morning.
  12. Line up the money and set a walk away number. The high bidder posts a nonrefundable deposit of 5 percent of the bid or $200, whichever is greater, and must pay the final bid plus documentary stamp tax and recording fees within 24 hours, excluding weekends and legal holidays.1 Subtract liens that survive, title clearing, repairs and holding costs from a realistic resale value before you bid.

FAQ

Does a Florida tax deed wipe out all liens?

No. Under Florida Statutes 197.552, most rights and interests do not survive the tax deed, but a lien of record held by a municipal or county governmental unit, special district, or community development district survives if it is not satisfied when the sale proceeds are disbursed.3

How much deposit is required at a Florida tax deed sale?

Florida Statutes 197.542(2) requires the high bidder to post a nonrefundable deposit of 5 percent of the bid or $200, whichever is greater. Many clerks require it to be funded before the sale, so check the county rules.1

When is full payment due after winning a Florida tax deed auction?

Under Florida Statutes 197.542(2), the final bid plus documentary stamp tax and recording fees must be paid within 24 hours, excluding weekends and legal holidays. Some clerks set an earlier clock time, such as 11 a.m. the next business day.1

Can the owner still redeem the property before the sale?

Yes. Florida Statutes 197.472 lets a person redeem a tax certificate at any time before a tax deed is issued, unless full payment for the tax deed has already been made to the clerk. Parcels can drop off the list up to sale day.4

Is a tax deed the same as insurable title?

Not necessarily. Many title insurers will not insure a tax deed without further steps such as a quiet title action. Ask a Florida title company or attorney about the specific parcel. This guide is research context, not legal or title advice.
